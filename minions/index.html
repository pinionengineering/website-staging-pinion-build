The problem: IPFS pinning runs on trust

IPFS is a powerful distribution protocol, but not a storage guarantee. When you pin content with a service, you are trusting them to keep it. There is no mechanism in the IPFS protocol to verify that a remote node is actually holding your data. The only way to confirm today is to re-download everything and recompute the entire Merkle tree from scratch — a cost that scales linearly with dataset size. For a multi-terabyte archive, this is operationally infeasible. In practice, it never happens.

A provider could silently lose blocks, cache only frequently-requested content, or fetch data on-demand when challenged, and you would never know. For AI training datasets, research archives, or any content where provenance and completeness matter, this is not good enough.

The Minion Network closes this gap. Storage nodes don’t ask for trust — they prove possession on demand.

Challenge us. We have to prove it.

The network continuously issues compact cryptographic challenges to every storage node. Each node must respond with a proof that it holds its assigned data. The entire exchange — challenge plus proof — is under 350 bytes, regardless of how large the dataset is. A petabyte-scale archive can be audited by exchanging a few hundred bytes.

Size doesn’t change the math

A single challenge samples uniformly across everything assigned to a node simultaneously — not just one file. At 10% data loss, the protocol detects it with 90–97% probability per round. Each round is independent, so detection compounds toward certainty over time. A node that has quietly lost data cannot keep passing random challenges drawn from the full block population.

This verification is not just something Pinion runs internally. It is a protocol that any authorized party can execute. If you need to confirm your data is intact, you can challenge the network yourself and evaluate the proof. The guarantee is mathematical, not contractual.

Actually distributed, not just distributed-adjacent

Most pinning services, regardless of what they call themselves, run out of a handful of cloud data centers under unified control. If the company goes down, or a provider cuts them off, your data goes with it.

The Minion Network is built on genuinely independent operators. Every dataset is assigned to multiple nodes selected for topological diversity — different networks, different physical locations, different administrative domains. No single organization controls a quorum of the nodes holding your content. This is what distributed storage actually means.

The assignment manager continuously monitors node health and automatically reassigns content away from degrading nodes — before availability is at risk. Operators who go offline lose assignments to better performers. The network self-heals.

For operators: honest storage is the most profitable strategy

The network pays you for every audit round you pass. But not all passes are equal — payment decays exponentially with response latency. Respond immediately, because you have the data locally, and you earn the full amount. Proxy challenged blocks from the network at the last second, and the latency penalty eats your margin. Over time, proxying costs more in lost payment than simply storing the data.

Reputation compounds this further. Every fast, valid response builds your reputation score, which multiplies your per-round earnings and makes you the first choice for new assignments. A single failed round causes a multiplicative penalty that drags down earnings on every subsequent round. Sustained failures trigger de-listing and loss of all assignments.

This is not a coincidence. The payment structure and reputation dynamics were designed from first principles so that honest, local, fast storage is the dominant strategy for every rational operator — not because we ask you to be honest, but because being honest pays the most.
